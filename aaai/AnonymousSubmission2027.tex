\begin{abstract}
Long-form video understanding must locate sparse evidence and reason over long-range spatio-temporal interactions under lossy sampling and limited context.
Recent agentic approaches build searchable caption as video indexes for iterative querying, but flat text and embeddings provide limited entity-relational structure and retrieval traceability.
We propose the \textbf{S}cene \textbf{G}raph-\textbf{D}riven Agent (SGDAgent), which converts open-ended caption search into structured candidate verification.
Specifically, we (1)~design a hierarchical spatio-temporal scene graph structure for fine-grained video semantics representation, (2)~build a bottom-up construction pipeline to generate such scene graphs, and (3)~design a scene graph-driven agent following a propose-verify-answer paradigm with graph-based retrieval tools to search video content in a structured way.
Experiments across four benchmarks demonstrate substantial improvements: SGDAgent achieves +18.2\,pp accuracy gain on LVBench with matched settings, and the structured graph index enables lighter backbones to match stronger baselines.
Agent trajectory analysis further shows that the gains arise from converting open retrieval into graph-anchored candidate verification.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

% - Para 1: Long-video understanding challenges -

% - Para 2: Current approaches and their limitations -

Long-form video understanding requires semantic analysis and reasoning over hour-scale videos and poses three fundamental challenges: \emph{incomplete coverage} from lossy frame sampling, \emph{imprecise reasoning} over long-range spatio-temporal entity interactions that flat embeddings or text fail to capture, and \emph{imprecise localization} of brief or cross-segment evidence buried in background noise.
Recent agentic video reasoning approaches~\cite{deepvideo2025,videoagent,drvideo2025} convert videos into caption-centric indexes and let LLMs iteratively query them.
Yet \emph{the evidence space remains fundamentally unstructured}: flat text and visual embeddings cannot represent high-order spatio-temporal semantics or support traceable retrieval.
Advances in multi-round retrieval~\cite{videoagent}, grounding-verification pipelines~\cite{videomind}, and multi-granularity toolkits~\cite{videoseek} improve \emph{how the agent searches} without restructuring \emph{what it searches over}.

% - Para 3: Scene graphs as structured representation -

Scene graphs encode visual entities and their spatio-temporal interactions as graph nodes and edges~\cite{scenegraph1,tpt2023}.
They provide the entity-level and relational grounding that flat representations lack.
Their structured form supports compact semantic modeling, explicit relational constraints, and precise retrieval through graph search~\cite{scenegraph1,scenegraph2,qiu2025step}.
Prior work has explored hierarchical interlacement graphs for video scene graph generation~\cite{hig2024}, topological semantic graphs for long-video reasoning~\cite{videomindpalace2025}, and entity-relation graphs for agentic frame selection~\cite{graphvideoagent2025,egagent2026}.
Other methods convert video frames into textual descriptions or simpler structured representations and build large-model agents for visual reasoning~\cite{fei2024videoofthought,videoagent,omagent2024,vagent2025,deepvideo2025,drvideo2025}.
However, two gaps prevent their direct application to long-form video QA\@.
First, existing methods focus on frame-level or segment-level local relations, lacking unified cross-scale hierarchical organization to capture both instantaneous details and cross-segment causal chains~\cite{tpt2023,vagent2025}.
Second, existing graph-aware systems either target graph generation, feed a graph directly to an LLM, or use a dynamic entity graph for frame selection, whereas SGDAgent uses a three-level scene graph as the primary retrieval index in a propose-verify-answer loop~\cite{hig2024,videomindpalace2025,graphvideoagent2025,egagent2026,vagent2025}.

% - Para 4: Our method -- representation + agent workflow -

In this work, we present the \textbf{S}cene \textbf{G}raph-\textbf{D}riven Agent (SGDAgent), a novel framework that transforms open-ended caption-based search into structured, graph-guided candidate verification.
Firstly, we devise a hierarchical spatio-temporal scene graph architecture that encodes fine-grained video semantics across multiple temporal scales. Then, we develop a bottom-up offline pipeline that constructs and indexes frame-level graphs, segment-level graphs, and a global event graph through a shared entity registry. Finally, we introduce a reasoning agent that uses graph retrieval to propose candidate regions and targeted visual inspection to verify them before answering.
SGDAgent therefore separates structured localization from visual confirmation within an interpretable propose-verify-answer process.

% - Para 5: Results -

These designs yield substantial gains under controlled settings.
On LVBench~\cite{lvbench}, augmenting the caption index with the three-level graph index raises end-to-end accuracy from 54.8\% to 64.4\% and answered accuracy from 65.7\% to 72.2\%.
Adding the routing, confidence-gating, and terminal-convergence strategies further raises end-to-end accuracy to 73.0\%, increases the answer rate from 83.5\% to 98.4\%, and reduces the failed-to-terminate (FTT) rate from 16.5\% to 1.6\%.
Across four benchmarks, SGDAgent with the lighter \texttt{o4-mini} backbone approaches DVD~\cite{deepvideo2025} with the stronger \texttt{o3} backbone on LVBench and LongVideoBench~\cite{longvideobench2024}, although these cross-method results are reference comparisons rather than fully controlled experiments.
Ablation and trajectory analyses show that segment-level graphs improve candidate discovery, the global event graph reduces search cost, and the control strategies improve answer quality and convergence.

% - Para 6: Contributions -

In summary, we contribute (1)~a \textbf{hierarchical spatio-temporal scene graph representation} comprising frame-level graphs, segment-level graphs, and a global event graph connected through a shared entity registry and constructed by a bottom-up offline pipeline; (2)~a \textbf{scene graph-driven agent} that combines graph-guided candidate proposal, visual verification, caption fallback, and uncertainty-aware control; and (3)~\textbf{comprehensive evaluation} on four benchmarks, including matched-setting ablations that separate the effects of graph indexing, graph depth, and agent control.



\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Long-video understanding.}
Vision foundation models and multimodal large language models have advanced short-clip understanding~\cite{radford2021CLIP,li2022GLIP,llava2023,video-llava2024,bai2025qwen3vl,caba2015activitynet}, but hour-scale videos still present three persistent challenges: large temporal spans, complex spatio-temporal interactions, and sparse critical evidence.
Long videos require discontinuous sampling~\cite{deepvideo2025} or token compression~\cite{wang2025adaretake}, which can lose detail~\cite{lvbench,longvideobench2024}, while text descriptions cannot explicitly capture long-range entity dependencies~\cite{zhang2024simplellm} and agents must progressively locate fleeting evidence~\cite{videoagent,omagent2024}.
Existing methods either extend context through longer windows, token compression, or hierarchical representations~\cite{gemini15,qwen,internvl,seed,radford2021CLIP,li2022GLIP,llava2023,video-llava2024,bai2025qwen3vl,longvu2025,shu2025video,apollo2025}, or retrieve evidence through selective sampling and agentic search~\cite{deepvideo2025,mrvideo,videoseek,zhang2024simplellm,videorag2025,videotree2025,drvideo2025,yang2025vca}.
These approaches reduce input scale or search cost, but sparse evidence must still be located within compressed or selectively sampled representations.
SGDAgent instead augments captions with a scene graph index that grounds candidate proposal in entities, relations, and temporal constraints.

\textbf{Scene graph representation.}
Scene graphs encode visual entities and their spatio-temporal interactions as graph nodes and edges~\cite{xu2017scene,tpt2023}.
Traditional detect-then-classify pipelines~\cite{xu2017scene,motifs2018} have evolved toward open-vocabulary generation with vision-language models~\cite{frompixel2024,vs32023,hyperglm2025}, although cross-segment association and temporal dynamics remain challenging.
HIG~\cite{hig2024} models hierarchical interlacement for video scene graph generation, while VideoMindPalace~\cite{videomindpalace2025} organizes long-video observations into an environment-grounded topological graph that is supplied to an LLM for reasoning.
GraphVideoAgent~\cite{graphvideoagent2025} maintains a dynamic entity-relation graph to guide frame selection, and EGAgent~\cite{egagent2026} combines temporally annotated entity scene graphs with structured visual and audio search over very long egocentric videos.
Together, these studies establish graph structure as useful for generation, memory organization, and evidence search in long videos.
SGDAgent differs by using a frame-segment-event scene graph as the retrieval index of a propose-verify-answer agent, where graph retrieval localizes candidates and targeted visual inspection confirms the facts.

\textbf{Agentic video reasoning.}
Directly feeding frame or clip tokens to language models~\cite{video-llava2024,videochat2025} is constrained by context length and computation on hour-scale videos.
Text-centered methods address this limitation through joint clip descriptions in LLoVi~\cite{zhang2024simplellm}, retrieval-augmented indexing in VideoRAG~\cite{videorag2025}, query-adaptive hierarchies in VideoTree~\cite{videotree2025}, or progressively refined document memory in DrVideo~\cite{drvideo2025}.
Interactive approaches include multi-round frame retrieval in VideoAgent~\cite{videoagent}, ReAct-based search over a multi-granularity database in DVD~\cite{deepvideo2025,react2022}, and human-like seeking tools in VideoSeek~\cite{videoseek}.
VideoMind~\cite{videomind} separates localization, verification, and answering, VCA~\cite{yang2025vca} uses curiosity-driven tree exploration, and Vgent~\cite{vagent2025} performs graph-structured retrieval and reasoning.
Other directions include multi-agent systems~\cite{omagent2024}, neuro-symbolic reasoning~\cite{neuro2020,cai2025naver}, and end-to-end post-training on agent trajectories~\cite{team2026kimi,zeng2026glm}.
These agents improve navigation flexibility, but vary in whether retrieved evidence is explicitly structured and independently verified.
SGDAgent complements these approaches with a scene graph hierarchy that proposes entity- and temporally grounded candidates for subsequent visual verification.


\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}


This section introduces the \textbf{S}cene \textbf{G}raph-\textbf{D}riven Agent (SGDAgent), which uses a \emph{hierarchical scene graph representation} to index multi-granular spatio-temporal video semantics and convert open-ended caption search into graph-anchored candidate verification.
Section~\ref{sec:representation} first defines the \textbf{hierarchical scene graph representation}, which forms the video index $D(V)=\{C(V),\;G^{f},\;G^{s},\;G^{g}\}$.
Section~\ref{sec:construction} then presents the \textbf{bottom-up scene graph construction} that extracts and aggregates the three graph levels offline.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:agent} describes the \textbf{scene graph-driven agent}, which runs an observe-reason-act loop under a \emph{propose-verify-answer} paradigm.

\subsection{Hierarchical Scene Graph Representation}
\label{sec:representation}

SGDAgent organizes video knowledge as a video index comprising a caption index and a three-level spatio-temporal scene graph representation (Figure~\ref{fig:graph}(c)):
\begin{equation}
  D(V) = \bigl\{C(V),\; G^{f},\; G^{s},\; G^{g}\bigr\},
\end{equation}
Here, $C(V)$ is the caption index for open semantic retrieval, $G^{f}=\{G_i^{f}\}_{i=1}^{N}$ is the frame-level graph sequence, $G^{s}=\{G_j^{s}\}_{j=1}^{M}$ is the segment-level graph sequence, and $G^{g}=(V^{g},\,\mathcal{E}^{g})$ is the global event graph with event nodes $V^{g}$ and edges $\mathcal{E}^{g}$.
All three levels share a video-level entity registry $E^{\mathrm{video}}$, in which each entry stores a unique ID, entity type, attributes, description, and temporal span from first to last appearance.
The registry therefore provides a cross-scale identity anchor.

\textbf{Frame-level graph.}
Each frame-level graph captures an entity-relation snapshot at a single keyframe:
\begin{equation}
  G_i^{f} = (E_i^{f},\; R_i^{f},\; t_i,\; c_i),
\end{equation}
Here, $E_i^{f} \subseteq E^{\mathrm{video}}$ is the visible entity set, $R_i^{f}$ contains relation triples $(s,p,o)$ with $s,o \in E_i^{f}$ and predicate $p$, $t_i$ is the timestamp, and $c_i$ is the aligned caption.
Frame-level graphs provide the finest spatial granularity by anchoring objects and local relations to specific time points.

\textbf{Segment-level graph.}
Each segment-level graph aggregates entity interactions over a short temporal window:
\begin{equation}
  G_j^{s} = (E_j^{s},\; R_j^{s},\;
  [t_j^{\mathrm{s}}, t_j^{\mathrm{e}}],\; F_j,\; s_j),
\end{equation}
Here, $E_j^{s}$ is the active entity set, $R_j^{s}$ contains temporal relations, $[t_j^{\mathrm{s}},t_j^{\mathrm{e}}]$ is the time range, $F_j$ contains supporting frame IDs, and $s_j$ is the segment summary.
Each temporal relation is represented as $(s,p,o,t_{\mathrm{start}},t_{\mathrm{end}},\mathcal{F})$, which binds its participants to a temporal extent and supporting frames.

\textbf{Global event graph.}
The global event graph $G^{g}=(V^{g},\,\mathcal{E}^{g})$ organizes higher-level event semantics over segments.
Each event node $n_k \in V^{g}$ is
\begin{equation}
  n_k = (P_k,\; R_k,\;
  [t_k^{\mathrm{s}}, t_k^{\mathrm{e}}],\; S_k,\; F_k,\; \sigma_k),
\end{equation}
Here, $P_k$ contains participant entities, $R_k$ contains salient relations, $[t_k^{\mathrm{s}},t_k^{\mathrm{e}}]$ is the event time range, $S_k$ contains supporting segment IDs, $F_k$ contains supporting frames, and $\sigma_k$ is the event summary.
Event nodes are typed as \emph{unit events} (compact episodes) or \emph{group events} (longer episodes).
Event edges $\mathcal{E}^{g}$ include \texttt{before}, \texttt{overlaps}, \texttt{entity-\allowbreak carryover}, \texttt{same-\allowbreak thread}, \texttt{parent-\allowbreak child}, encoding cross-segment chains and hierarchical structure.


\subsection{Bottom-up Scene Graph Construction}
\label{sec:construction}

We develop a bottom-up pipeline to generate the hierarchical scene graph representation, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:graph}(b).
Stage~1 constructs frame-level graphs queried by $FR$, Stage~2 aggregates them into segment-level graphs queried by $SR$, and Stage~3 organizes the segments into the global event graph queried by $GR$.

\textbf{Stage~1: Frame-level graph extraction and entity registration.}
Keyframes are uniformly sampled at fixed intervals.
For each keyframe, a VLM produces a structured scene graph in a single pass, including typed entities, attributes, and relation triples.
Entity identity is maintained by greedily aligning each new entity to the video-level registry $E^{\mathrm{video}}$ with a weighted score $0.7R_{\mathrm{name}}+0.2R_{\mathrm{desc}}+0.1R_{\mathrm{type}}$.
Entities with scores of at least $\tau=0.78$ are merged, while the remaining entities are registered as new.
When the VLM extracts no usable entity, coarse-grained entities are recovered from temporally neighboring captions to prevent empty-\allowbreak graph gaps.

\textbf{Stage~2: Segment-level graph merging.}
Frame-level graphs are aggregated into segment-level graphs through 30-second sliding windows with 5-second overlap.
Within each window, frame-level triples with the same $(s,p,o)$ signature are merged into temporal relations spanning the earliest to the latest supporting frame.
Frame IDs and occurrence counts are retained.
Each segment-level graph also includes a textual summary, while overlap links between adjacent windows support cross-\allowbreak boundary retrieval.

\textbf{Stage~3: Global event graph construction.}
Temporally proximate and semantically coherent segments are clustered into events.
Two adjacent segments are mergeable when their temporal gap is at most 12 seconds and they satisfy a semantic condition based on relation overlap, shared entities, or predicate overlap.
The relation- and predicate-overlap thresholds are 0.18 and 0.22, respectively.
Overly long or discontinuous clusters are split into unit events, and multiple units form group events through \texttt{parent-\allowbreak child} edges.
Pairwise \texttt{before}, \texttt{overlaps}, \texttt{entity-\allowbreak carryover}, and \texttt{same-\allowbreak thread} edges are inferred from temporal order and entity sharing.

\textbf{Quality control.}
Lightweight constraints are applied throughout the pipeline: an entity blacklist removes generic entities, a predicate whitelist suppresses noisy relations, and schema validation enforces structural consistency.
In the global event graph, confidence and uncertainty estimates are attached to nodes for downstream gating.


\subsection{Scene Graph-Driven Agent}
\label{sec:agent}

\begin{figure*}[!tb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{../paper/figures/workflow.pdf}
  \caption{Scene graph-driven agent. A long video is converted into a video index comprising frame-level graphs, segment-level graphs, a global event graph, and a caption index. An adaptive reasoning agent searches this index through a propose-verify tool chain within an observe-reason-act loop.}
  \label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure*}


\begin{algorithm}[!tb]
  \caption{SGDAgent: Graph-Driven Reasoning Loop}
  \label{alg:search}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \footnotesize
  \REQUIRE Video index $D(V)=\{C(V), G^f, G^s, G^g\}$, query $q$,
    budget $T$
  \ENSURE Answer $\hat{a}$
  \STATE $H \leftarrow \textsc{InitState}(q)$
  \/\/ routing state, candidates, evidence
  \FOR{$t = 1, \ldots, T$}
      \STATE $a_t \leftarrow \textsc{LLM}(H)$
        \/\/ $a_t \in \{GR, SR, FR, FI, \textsc{Answer}\}$
      \IF{$a_t \in \{GR, SR, FR\}$}
          \STATE $\mathcal{W} \leftarrow \textsc{QueryGraph}(a_t, q, D(V))$
          \IF{$\textsc{WeakSignal}(\mathcal{W}, H)$}
              \STATE $H \leftarrow \textsc{CaptionBackoff}(H, q, C(V))$
          \ELSE
              \STATE $H \leftarrow \textsc{UpdateCandidates}(H, \mathcal{W})$
          \ENDIF
      \ELSIF{$a_t = FI$}
          \STATE $e \leftarrow \textsc{FrameInspect}(\textsc{Top}(H), q)$
          \STATE $H \leftarrow \textsc{UpdateEvidence}(H, e)$
      \ELSIF{$a_t = \textsc{Answer} \land \textsc{GatingOK}(H)$}
          \STATE \RETURN $\textsc{Readout}(H)$
      \ELSE
          \STATE $H \leftarrow \textsc{RejectFinish}(H)$
      \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
  \STATE \RETURN $\textsc{ForcedAnswer}(H)$
  \end{algorithmic}
  \end{algorithm}

The reasoning agent uses the prebuilt hierarchical spatio-temporal scene graph to index multi-granular video semantics, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}.

\textbf{Adaptive reasoning agent loop.}
The agent operates in a closed loop following the observe-reason-act paradigm~\cite{react2022} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:search}).
At each step~$t$, the agent \emph{observes} the current state~$H$, comprising the question, retrieved candidates, accumulated evidence, and control signals from prior tool calls.
The backbone LLM then \emph{reasons} over~$H$ and selects an action $a_t \in \mathcal{T}$.
The selected tool executes, and its output---candidate windows, visual observations, or controller signals---updates~$H$ for the next iteration.
\textsc{Answer} is accepted only when the accumulated evidence passes confidence gating.
For every claim derived from graph retrieval, the gate requires qualified visual evidence from $FI$ rather than accepting graph text as a final fact.
If the budget~$T$ is exhausted without convergence, a forced readout extracts the best-supported option from the trajectory.

\textbf{Tool chain.}\quad
% NOTE on GR vs.\ current implementation: the action set below reflects the
% target scene-graph architecture. In the present online system, a full GR
% over a true global event graph is not yet realised as a callable tool; the
% global event graph is approximated by (i) a Global Event Graph Index---a prompt-injected
% timeline summary pre-computed from the segment-level graph---and (ii) a
% caption-based global-browse helper. Upgrading this to a tool-level query
% over a complete global event graph is consistent with the hierarchy in
% Sec.~3.1 and is left as future work.
Following the propose-verify design in Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}, the agent acts over $\mathcal{T}=\{GR,\,\allowbreak SR,\,\allowbreak FR,\,\allowbreak FI,\,\allowbreak CB,\,\allowbreak \textsc{Answer}\}$.
The graph retrieval tools $GR$, $SR$, and $FR$ query the global event graph, segment-level graphs, and frame-level graphs, respectively, to propose candidates at progressively finer granularity.
The pixel-inspection tool $FI$ verifies these proposals against raw frames, while the caption backoff $CB$ supplies an orthogonal dense-text channel.
Each proposal call returns both ranked regions and a vector of \emph{controller signals} that estimates how its output should be used.
These signals let the agent build a confidence-aware cascade rather than blindly fuse retrieval scores.

At the interface level, the tool chain can be summarized as
\[
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$
  \begin{array}{rcl}
  \textsc{GraphRetrieve}_{\ell}: & (q,\ \mathrm{scope},\ k) & \mapsto\ (\mathrm{regions}_{1:k},\ \mathrm{signals}), \\
  \textsc{VerifyPixels}: & (\mathrm{window},\ q) & \mapsto\ \mathrm{evidence}, \\
  \textsc{CaptionBackoff}: & (q) & \mapsto\ \mathrm{summary}.
  \end{array}
$}
\]
All three graph retrievers share a common scorer that ranks a candidate region $w$ against the query $q$ by
\begin{equation}
  S(w \mid q) \;=\; S_{\mathrm{lex}}(w,q) \;+\;
  S_{\mathrm{soft}}(w,q) \;+\; S_{\mathrm{temp}}(w,q),
\end{equation}
Here, $S_{\mathrm{lex}}$ aggregates token overlap scored separately over entity names, relation predicates, and region summaries.
$S_{\mathrm{soft}}$ is the cosine similarity between embeddings when region vectors are available and falls back to lexical fuzzy matching otherwise.
$S_{\mathrm{temp}}$ combines the intersection over union with any time span parsed from $q$ and soft anchors inferred from cues such as \emph{beginning}, \emph{end}, or \emph{middle}.
A build-time entity blacklist keeps video-production artifacts such as ``scene'', ``shot'', and ``narrator'', as well as mis-extracted verbs, out of both $S_{\mathrm{lex}}$ and $S_{\mathrm{soft}}$.
The $\mathrm{scope}$ argument carries a time constraint forwarded from a coarser-level call.
Each returned region also carries a \emph{provenance pointer} to supporting nodes at the next finer level, which provides the structure for cascaded retrieval.

\emph{Global event graph retrieval (GR).}\quad
$GR$ queries the global event graph and is the coarsest proposer and natural entry point for causal, plot-level, and other video-spanning questions without a prior scope.
A call takes the question and the entire video timeline as its search space, then returns a short list of event blocks spanning tens of seconds to minutes.
Each result includes event-to-event relations and provenance pointers to its supporting segments.
The agent consumes these outputs in two ways.
Each event's time span is forwarded as the $\mathrm{scope}$ of a subsequent $SR$ call, narrowing segment-level retrieval from the whole video to a few minutes in one step.
Independently, the event-to-event chain dictates the order in which events are visited when the question requires following a causal or temporal sequence, turning what would be a flat multi-hop search into a directed walk over the global event graph.

\emph{Segment-level graph retrieval (SR).}\quad
$SR$ is the primary proposer during online inference and is invoked either without a prior scope or within the scope of a preceding $GR$ result.
It indexes sliding segments of roughly $10$--$30$ seconds, each aggregating entities and time-stamped temporal triples over a short window.
A call returns ranked segment windows, their highest-scoring temporal triples, provenance pointers to supporting keyframes, and the full controller-signal vector.
The agent uses $SR$'s time windows as the default input to $FI$ whenever pixel verification is warranted, and drills down through the provenance pointers to $FR$ when object- or relation-level grounding is needed.
Because $SR$'s output is structured, its controller signals can report quality along several axes at once: whether the top hit was only soft-matched, whether the best window is too wide for the question, and whether the question type demands visual detail the graph cannot settle on its own.

\emph{Frame-level graph retrieval (FR).}\quad
$FR$ is the finest-grained proposer and is reserved for questions that need object-level grounding: specific attributes, identity, counts, or pairwise spatial relations at a particular moment.
It is invoked with a narrow $\mathrm{scope}$ forwarded from $SR$ and returns a ranked set of keyframes, each carrying an entity--attribute--relation snapshot anchored to a single timestamp.
Its output plays two roles.
The keyframe timestamps provide tightly bounded inputs to $FI$, limiting pixel sampling to windows of a few seconds.
The returned entities and relations remain candidate evidence and cannot serve as terminal facts until $FI$ confirms the relevant visual content.

\emph{Pixel verification (FI) and caption backoff (CB).}\quad
$FI$ is the only action that returns to raw pixels.
It consumes a graph-proposed time window, uniformly samples frames within it, and performs question-conditioned visual QA over those frames.
The resulting evidence is bound to a narrow, graph-localized region and can support the final answer.
$CB$ instead queries the embedding-based caption index and returns a dense natural-language summary of matching clips when the graph index lacks the surface form used by a question.
Caption backoff supplements candidate discovery but does not validate the correctness of graph-derived claims.

\emph{Controller-signal routing.}\quad
The controller signals emitted by each graph retrieval turn these interfaces into a coordinated cascade.
They summarize whether the top hit was lexical or fuzzy-only, whether the best region is too coarse, whether the question requires visual detail, and whether the retriever recommends descending another level or invoking pixel verification.
\textbf{Strategy~R} (entry routing) maps counts, colors, and on-screen text directly to $FI$, routes causal and plot-level questions through $GR$, and starts localization questions from $SR$.
\textbf{Strategy~G} (confidence gating) blocks \textsc{Answer} when a \emph{localize-only} or \emph{weak-match} signal is raised, escalates coarse candidates for fine-detail questions to narrow-window visual verification, and redirects weak graph retrieval to $CB$ when appropriate.
The finish gate additionally requires qualified $FI$ evidence before any graph-derived claim can support \textsc{Answer}.
\textbf{Strategy~T} (terminal convergence) extracts the best-supported option from the accumulated trace when the budget $T$ is exhausted, preventing otherwise useful trajectories from ending as FTT outcomes.
The resulting coarse-to-fine cascade qualifies each retrieval hop through controller signals instead of blindly fusing all scores.
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} evaluates R, G, and T separately.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Dataset.}
Our primary benchmark is \textbf{LVBench}~\cite{lvbench}, which contains 1{,}549 four-choice video QA questions over long-form videos.
The system receives a video, a natural-language question, and four candidate answers, and it must return exactly one option.
Failure to return a valid option is recorded as a failed-to-terminate (FTT) outcome.

\textbf{Implementation details.}
The reasoning backbone is \texttt{o4-mini} (temperature~0) with a maximum of 15 reasoning steps per question.
Stage~1 extracts frame-level graphs by prompting \texttt{gpt-4.1} on keyframes sampled at one frame every 5~seconds (up to 120 per video).
Stage~2 constructs segment-level graphs using 30-second sliding windows with 5-second overlap.
Local visual verification is performed by \texttt{gpt-4.1-mini}, with a budget of 50~frames per \texttt{frame\_inspect} call.
$GR$ returns at most 5 candidates from the global event graph, $SR$ returns 6 from the segment-level graphs, and $FR$ returns 8 from the frame-level graphs.
Building the scene graph index for all 103 LVBench videos takes approximately 7.94 hours of wall-clock time with four parallel workers, while Stage~3, which constructs the global event graph, takes approximately 121 seconds.
The video index is constructed once and reused across all questions.
To ensure a fair matched-setting comparison, all internally compared methods share the same reasoning backbone, frame-inspection VLM, caption source, video index, candidate answer set, and budget limits.
All methods are permitted to produce FTT outcomes.

\textbf{Evaluation metrics.}
We report four complementary metrics: \emph{end-to-end accuracy} (e2e\_acc), the fraction of all questions answered correctly; \emph{answered accuracy} (ans\_acc), the accuracy over questions for which the system produced a valid option; \emph{answer rate}, the fraction of questions that received a valid option; and \emph{FTT rate}, the fraction of questions for which the system failed to return an option because of empty output, runtime failure, or trajectory non-convergence.

\subsection{Main Results}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Performance positioning.}
Table~\ref{tab:main_comparison} positions SGDAgent against published results on four long-video understanding benchmarks.
Because the methods differ in backbone, caption source, and evaluation protocol, these numbers are reference comparisons rather than controlled evidence of component effectiveness.
The strict conclusions of this paper therefore rest on the matched-setting LVBench results and ablations in Section~\ref{sec:ablation}.

With \texttt{o4-mini}, SGDAgent achieves 73.0\% on LVBench and 71.2\% on LongVideoBench.
These results approach the reported DVD~\cite{deepvideo2025} scores of 74.2\% and 71.6\%, respectively, obtained with the stronger \texttt{o3} backbone.
With \texttt{o3}, SGDAgent obtains reported scores of 75.9\% on LVBench, 74.0\% on LongVideoBench, 69.0\% on Video-MME Long~\cite{videomme2024}, and 77.4\% on EgoSchema~\cite{egoschema2023}.
The corresponding DVD results are 74.2\%, 71.6\%, 67.3\%, and 76.6\%.
These reference positions are consistent with the matched-setting finding that structured candidate proposal can complement the reasoning backbone, but they are not used to isolate the source of the gain.

\subsection{Analysis on Agentic Reasoning Behavior}
\label{sec:analysis}


The ablation results show \emph{what} improves, while this section examines \emph{how} search behavior changes by comparing the reasoning trajectories of the caption-only baseline and SGDAgent (Figure~\ref{fig:trajectory}).
The key finding is that graph-driven proposal replaces open-ended caption exploration with a focused cascaded narrowing, producing trajectories that are both shorter and more targeted.

\textbf{Case study: escaping the frame-inspect trap.}\quad
The question asks: ``Why does the man follow the woman in red after they meet for the third time?'' (correct answer: the woman has his book).
The question is challenging because the agent must first locate a specific \emph{repeated event} (the third encounter) within an hour-long video and then infer the \emph{motivation} behind the subsequent action from an object-possession relation.
The caption-only baseline follows $\mathrm{GB} \!\to\! \mathrm{FI}^{\times 5} \!\to\! \mathrm{Answer}$ and selects the wrong option.
Without a structured intermediate representation, the agent cannot reliably connect ``the man,'' ``the woman in red,'' ``the third encounter,'' and ``the carried object'' into a coherent chain.
It therefore performs repeated broad-range frame inspections that return noisy local descriptions until the budget is exhausted and an answer is forced from fragmentary evidence.
This is the \emph{frame-inspect trap}~\cite{deepvideo2025}.

SGDAgent follows $\mathrm{GR} \!\to\! \mathrm{SR} \!\to\! \mathrm{FR} \!\to\! \mathrm{FI} \!\to\! \mathrm{Answer}$, with each graph level progressively narrowing the candidate space.
$GR$ first anchors ``the third encounter'' to event \texttt{evt\_0055} [23:45--24:40] with high confidence.
This result reduces the search scope from the full video to a 55-second window containing the relevant participants and the action ``follow.''
$SR$ localizes to segment \texttt{seg\_0058} [23:45--24:15], surfacing active entities (woman, man, building) and their spatial relations within the event window.
$FR$ queries ``woman book'' at the frame level and retrieves \emph{person--holding--book} triples from keyframes \texttt{fra\_0064} and \texttt{fra\_0086}.
These triples provide an explicit hypothesis for the subsequent visual check.
A single focused $FI$ call on the narrowed window [24:00--24:15] confirms the book-possession relation and yields the correct answer.
In contrast, the baseline consumes five $FI$ calls spread across the entire video yet never assembles these cues into a coherent chain.

The graph does not directly answer the question but progressively converts an open-ended ``why'' query into verification over a small candidate set.
Global event graph retrieval narrows the temporal scope, segment-level graph retrieval surfaces entity interactions, and frame-level graph matching identifies the object relation that $FI$ must confirm.
This demonstrates that gains stem from restructuring search itself rather than from stronger frame inspection.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presents SGDAgent, which restructures long-form video understanding through a video index that combines captions with hierarchical spatio-temporal scene graphs.
Its core components are a three-level scene graph representation connected through a shared entity registry, a bottom-up construction pipeline, and a graph-driven agent that follows the propose-verify-answer paradigm.
Under matched LVBench settings~\cite{lvbench}, augmenting captions with the graph index raises end-to-end accuracy from 54.8\% to 64.4\% and answered accuracy from 65.7\% to 72.2\%.
The routing, confidence-gating, and terminal-convergence strategies further raise end-to-end accuracy to 73.0\% and the answer rate to 98.4\%.
Ablations show that segment-level graphs drive the accuracy improvement through structured candidate discovery, while the global event graph primarily improves efficiency through cascaded retrieval.
Trajectory analysis confirms that the graph narrows the candidate space and that targeted visual inspection verifies the final evidence before answering.
